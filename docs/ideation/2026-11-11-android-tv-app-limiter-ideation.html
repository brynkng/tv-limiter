Top pick: I1, one rule model where the whole TV is a pool and each app limit is a cap inside it. It is the decision your main feature and your whole-TV feature both rest on, and once it exists, everything else (warnings, PIN grants, history, bedtime) is written once instead of twice.

Grounding Context

Topic Context

What is being built. A personal or family Android TV app modeled on TVGuard. In your priority order: (1) pick an installed app and set a daily limit; (2) a weekday schedule so each day can have its own limit; (3) at time-up, a confirmation window with a PIN override; (4) PIN entry masked from onlookers using D-pad arrows; (5) whole-TV limits with the same options. You left it open with "what else?".

Still unsettled. What an override grants (rest of day or a fixed extension), warnings before expiry, the reset hour, who holds the PIN (a parent, or you limiting yourself), bedtime windows, how app and whole-TV limits interact, and how a forgotten PIN is recovered.

Outside evidence. TVGuard pairs a TV app with a phone app, tracks with Usage Access, and uses Device Admin so it can't be removed without the PIN. Google TV kids profiles have daily limits but reportedly no per-app limits, and limits stop at one device. Fire TV has reported holes: a settings page reachable without the PIN, and a factory reset that starts without it. On Sony TVs, a forgotten PIN means a factory reset. The usual way to detect the foreground app is polling UsageStatsManager from a foreground service. Detection is unreliable on some TV builds, and the overlay permission screens can be missing there. Known bypasses: changing the clock or time zone, rebooting, force stop, clearing data, cached sessions, launching from other apps, ADB, and using a second device. On PINs, research on moving-cursor D-pad entry (Springer, 10.1007/s10015-023-00890-y) and scrambled keypads (arXiv 1908.09165) agrees: a fixed arrow sequence can be learned by watching, and the protection comes from changing the mapping every time.

I1. One rule model: whole-TV pool, per-app caps inside it

Limit rules and schedulesConfidence 85%Complexity Medium
Description
Build one kind of limit, not two. A limit has a scope (one app, a group of apps, or the whole TV), a seven-day table of minutes, a reset hour, and an override policy. Every minute in an app counts against every limit that covers it, and whichever runs out first blocks. The block screen names the limit that fired ("YouTube is done for today" vs "TV time is used up"), and the PIN override applies to that limit only. Groups such as "all streaming apps share 90 minutes" come almost for free.
Basis
direct: "Let's also put in general TV limits. For with the same customization options." The same options mean the same data shape. The grounding lists "how per-app and whole-TV limits interact" as unsettled. external: corporate cards have an overall limit plus caps per spending category, and a charge must clear both.
Rationale
If the two limit types are built as separate timers, every later feature needs two versions, and users get blocks they can't explain (the app still has time but the TV is locked). One model with a "tightest wins" rule answers the open interaction question and makes groups, bedtime windows, and profiles new scope values instead of new systems.
Downsides
More design up front than a single per-app counter. Overlapping limits need a clear on-screen explanation, or the "which rule fired" message becomes confusing.

I2. A scrambled arrow pad that reveals nothing to a watcher

PIN override and masked entryConfidence 85%Complexity Low
Description
Keep your "arrows for the numbers" idea and add the part that makes it secure. The entry field shows only dots. A digit strip is on screen, but the cursor starts on a random digit each time, jumps to a new random digit after each OK press, and the digit order is reshuffled on every entry. Someone watching sees "left, left, up, OK", which tells them nothing. Build it as one component and reuse it for the override, opening settings, changing the PIN, and recovery.
Basis
direct: "the technique where it like just shows like arrows for the numbers" (your request). external: the moving-cursor D-pad scheme in Springer 10.1007/s10015-023-00890-y, where "the cursor's position changes after each press so the chosen digit is not revealed", and scrambled keypads (arXiv 1908.09165). direct: "a plain D-pad sequence as the PIN is observable; the protection comes from randomizing the mapping each time."
Rationale
Read literally, an arrow-sequence PIN can be learned by a kid sitting on the couch in a couple of evenings. Randomizing the mapping each time is what makes the masking you asked for actually work, and building it once protects every PIN entry point in the app.
Downsides
Entry is slower than a fixed keypad. Research on scrambled keypads confirms the slowdown, so test whether the family tolerates it. This adopts the published scheme rather than inventing a new one.

I3. The override grants a chosen amount, optionally borrowed from tomorrow

PIN override and masked entryConfidence 80%Complexity Low
Description
A correct PIN doesn't just switch the limit off. It opens a short menu: +15 min, +30 min, finish this episode, or rest of day. Each limit's policy decides which options appear, and there's a daily cap on grants. An optional "borrow" setting takes the minutes from tomorrow's allowance, like the emergency credit on a prepaid electricity meter, and says so on screen: "Unlocking adds 30 min today. Tomorrow drops from 2h to 1h30." Every grant is recorded.
Basis
direct: "Unsettled: what a PIN override grants (rest of day vs fixed extension)". external: prepaid meters give a fixed emergency credit that the next top-up pays back first. The grounding also mentions Nintendo letting a player finish the current game.
Rationale
If the override can only grant everything, the PIN holder learns to say no, or one PIN entry ends limiting for the day. Small, visible grants make "yes" cheap without making the limit meaningless. Borrowing keeps the weekly total honest, which also suits the case where you are limiting yourself.
Downsides
Another screen after the PIN. Borrowing needs clear wording, or tomorrow's lower limit feels like a bug.

I4. A weekday grid built for the remote

Setup and visibilityConfidence 80%Complexity Medium
Description
The app picker lists apps by actual recent usage (Usage Access already provides it), so the heavy apps are at the top. Picking one opens a single screen of seven bars: left and right pick a day, up and down change minutes in 15-minute steps, and a long press on OK offers "copy to weekdays" or "copy to weekend". The same grid handles the whole-TV limit. Like a programmable thermostat, a change made from the block screen asks "today only, or change the schedule?".
Basis
direct: "being able to set a schedule for which days of the week gets which time limit" (your request). external: seven-day programmable thermostats solved per-day settings with only a few buttons, using day groups, copy, and "temporary hold" vs "permanent change". TVGuard moves setup to a phone, which suggests setup with a remote is painful.
Rationale
Your main feature is seven numbers per app. If you enter them one field at a time with a D-pad, people give up and keep the defaults. One grid with copy shortcuts turns that into one or two edits.
Downsides
A custom TV control has to be built and tuned for focus behavior. Fifteen-minute steps may feel too coarse for small limits.

I5. Wind down instead of cutting off

The time-up momentConfidence 65%Complexity Medium
Description
Opening an app shows a small "40 min left today" badge, so time-up isn't a surprise. If the budget is already empty, the app is blocked as soon as it's detected. That also catches cached sessions and launches from other apps. Corner warnings appear at 10, 5, and 1 minute. At zero there's a short capped grace (for example 5 minutes) before the PIN window appears. Where it can be detected, the grace ends early when playback pauses or the episode ends.
Basis
direct: the grounding names "warning countdown before block" as a gap, and lists "cached sessions reopening an app, launching via intents from other apps" as bypasses. external: Nintendo Switch parental controls choose between alarm only and suspending the software, and let a player finish the current game.
Rationale
An abrupt mid-scene cut is when people demand the PIN or look for a bypass. A predictable wind-down makes your confirmation window rarer and less of a fight.
Downsides
Detecting a natural break means reading other apps' media sessions, which needs notification-listener access, and many TV apps don't report usable state. The capped grace has to be the default, with the smart ending as a bonus. Polling has a lag of under a second, so "block before anything shows" is really "block almost immediately". The grace could become a loophole if it isn't capped.
Check on real hardware before you commit: whether the overlay permission and notification-listener access can be granted on your specific TV.

I6. A usage log that charges and records every blind spot

Enforcement and tamper resistanceConfidence 65%Complexity Medium
Description
Keep one append-only record of viewing sessions, timed by the monotonic clock (elapsed time since boot) and checked against network time when available. The remaining-time badge, warnings, history, and limit checks are all read from it. The day resets only when the wall clock moves forward and that much elapsed time has really passed, so setting the clock back never refills the budget. After a reboot, force stop, or crash, the app replays the system's usage events to fill the gap, and time it still can't account for counts as used. The same record holds a short "things that look off" list that you see after a correct PIN: clock moved back, tracking off for 52 minutes, usage permission revoked.
Basis
direct: the bypasses listed in the grounding: "changing clock or time zone, reboot resetting timers, ... force stop, clear data". external: "Mitigations: monotonic clock plus network time, boot receiver". Flight recorders and tamper-evident seals rely on detection rather than prevention. reasoned: a sideloaded app can't block every bypass, but making each one costly and visible deters most of them.
Rationale
Changing the clock is the easiest bypass a kid will find, and it sits in plain TV settings. A limit that quietly stops working is worse than none. One record that charges and logs the gaps covers a whole group of bypasses at once, and history comes for free.
Downsides
Android only keeps usage events for days, not weeks, and they can be sparse on some TV builds, so replay is a best effort. Nothing survives clearing data or a factory reset unless Device Admin blocks those. "Unexplained time counts as used" will sometimes charge for a genuine crash.

I7. One-time override codes and recovery codes from your phone

PIN override and masked entryConfidence 60%Complexity Medium
Description
Alongside the static PIN, the override accepts a one-time code. The simple version: pair a standard authenticator app on your phone by scanning a QR code at setup, then type the current 6-digit code on the scrambled pad. The stronger version: the block screen shows a short challenge number, and your phone turns that number plus a chosen grant ("30 min") into a reply code. The reply works once, only for that challenge, and can be read out over a phone call when you're away. At setup the app also shows a few single-use recovery codes, once, to print and keep, so a forgotten PIN never means a factory reset.
Basis
direct: "one-time codes instead of a static PIN" and "remote 'ask for more time'" are named gaps. direct: "Sony TVs: forgotten PIN requires a factory reset (bad recovery design)." external: bank hardware tokens, standard TOTP authenticators, and two-factor backup codes.
Rationale
Masking protects one entry. A one-time code makes an observed entry worthless. Today, when the parent is out, the PIN gets texted to the kid and is leaked for good. This works offline and needs no server. The recovery codes settle the forgotten-PIN question in the same step.
Downsides
The challenge version needs a small phone-side tool to compute replies. The plain TOTP version avoids that but can't carry a grant amount. If the app is only for limiting yourself, this adds little.
